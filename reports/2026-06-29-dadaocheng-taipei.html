🍜 Where to Eat

Yi Tiáo Lóng (一條龍) — Braised Pork Rice

One of the oldest operating restaurants in Taipei, sitting on the southern edge of the Dihua Street commercial loop. Founded in 1946, Yi Tiáo Lóng has been serving the same braised pork rice (lǔ ròu fàn) for 80 years. Cash only, communal seating, no menus — you sit down and food appears. Dihua Street regulars, herb traders, and tourists have been sharing the same tables since before Taiwan's economic miracle.

Order: Braised pork rice (滷肉飯) + braised egg + pork intestine soup. Under $3 USD. Eat standing up if you have to.
📜 Historical Fact

The Oldest Commercial Street in Taipei — Built in the 1850s

Dihua Street dates to the 1850s, when Quanzhou merchants relocated from the Bangka district and built what was then called "Centre Street." For the next 150 years it was the wholesale backbone of Taiwan — Chinese medicinal herbs, dried goods, fabrics, incense, and Taiwanese tea all flowed through here before going anywhere else on the island.

During the Japanese colonial period (1895–1945) the street's distinctive baroque and Southern Fujian shophouse architecture was built — the same red-brick façades you'll walk past today. The city almost demolished them in the 1990s to build a road. A preservation movement stopped it. Dihua Street is now one of the few places in Taipei where you can walk through 19th-century commercial architecture that's still operating as commercial architecture.

The number: 750,000 people visit Dihua Street in the two weeks before Chinese New Year alone. Annual transactions on the street exceed $3 billion USD.
🏗️ Future Development Signal

Dadaocheng Wharf Waterfront Redevelopment — Tamsui River Revival

The Dadaocheng Wharf on the Tamsui River — once Taipei's primary port and the entry point for all goods coming into 19th-century Taiwan — is undergoing a deliberate cultural revival. Taipei City has been investing in waterfront activation: seasonal markets, pop-up cultural events, and new riverside park infrastructure connecting Dadaocheng to the broader Datong District redevelopment zone.

The signal: boutique hotels, creative studios, and design brands have been quietly moving into the Dihua Street side streets over the past five years — the same gentrification pattern that transformed Tokyo's Yanaka and Seoul's Ikseon-dong. The street is navigating the tension between preservation and commercialization in real time. Property values in Datong District have risen steadily as a result.

The bet: Dadaocheng is in the early-middle stage of becoming Taipei's most culturally significant tourism district — after decades of being overlooked in favor of Xinyi and Zhongshan. The window to experience it before full tourist saturation is probably 3–5 years.

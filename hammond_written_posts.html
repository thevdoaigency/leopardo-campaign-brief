Week 1 — The Headline / What Hammond Did

Most cities fix infrastructure the way you'd eat an elephant. One bite. One budget cycle. One line item that survives the council meeting.

Hammond, Indiana ate the whole thing.

76 new vehicles.
4,800 city-owned streetlights converted to LED.
HVAC replaced in 13 buildings.
119 high-mileage vehicles converted to run on propane.

All of it at once. All of it under one contract.

Here's the part that breaks people's brains: Hammond didn't raise taxes to do it. They didn't issue a bond. They didn't touch the general fund.

The project is structured so the savings it generates cover the debt payment on the work itself. The city writes no check.

Mayor Thomas McDermott put it in three words: "Cheaper. Cleaner. Nicer."

Over the next eight weeks we're going to walk through exactly how this was built — the four phases, the guarantee, the fleet conversion, and the number at the center of it that we still have to say slowly for people to believe.

Because Hammond didn't just avoid a cost.

They created a revenue stream.

Most cities fix one thing at a time.

It's the responsible-sounding choice. Do the roof this year. The boiler next year. The fleet when something finally dies in a parking lot in February.

It's also the reason the list never gets shorter.

Here's what nobody says out loud: fixing one thing at a time is the most expensive way to fix anything. You pay mobilization four times. You pay engineering four times. And the savings from any single project are never big enough to pay for the project — so you're always asking for money you don't have.

Hammond, Indiana did four scopes at once. Buildings. Streetlights. Propane. Fleet.

Not because they're reckless.

Because that's the only scale at which the math flips — where the savings get large enough to fund the work itself.

Going big wasn't the risk.

Going small, one year at a time, forever — that was the risk.

Week 2 — The Model / What an ESPC Is
REEL THIS WEEK: R2 · Rob defines the ESPC + VO · LI-CO
LI-CO Wed Leopardo Efficiency — Company Page
195 WORDS

"Guaranteed energy savings performance contract."

Five words that sound like a procurement form and are actually the reason Hammond, Indiana has 76 new vehicles.

Here's the plain version.

A normal contractor tells you what a project costs. You find the money. If the savings never show up, that's your problem.

An ESPC inverts it.

We audit your buildings and your fleet. We identify what's wasting money. Then we tell you what the upgrades will save — and we put that number in a contract with our name on it.

If the savings don't materialize, we write the check for the difference. Not you.

That guarantee is what makes the financing work. A lender will fund a project against a savings stream somebody is contractually on the hook for. They won't fund a hope.

So the sequence runs: we guarantee the savings → the guarantee makes the project financeable → the financing means the city pays nothing up front → the savings cover the debt service.

Rob Vollrath, who runs Leopardo Efficiency, says it in one line: we specialize in guaranteed energy savings performance contracting for local government agencies.

The important word is the first one.

Week 3 — The Four Phases / Scope
REEL THIS WEEK: R3 · Jeff — four phases · IG
LI-CO Wed Leopardo Efficiency — Company Page
188 WORDS

Jeff Sauline, our project manager on Hammond, describes the job in one sentence: "There's four phases of the project. We've got the building work, the street lighting, the propane upgrades, and the vehicle leasing program."

Four phases. One contract. Here's why that matters more than it sounds.

Any one of those scopes, standing alone, is a hard sell.

Building HVAC saves real money — but slowly. Streetlights save fast and cheap, but the total is small. Fleet costs a fortune up front and the savings take years. Propane conversion is the best return of the four, and nobody funds it because it doesn't feel urgent.

Alone, each one loses the budget fight. Together, they don't.

The streetlights and propane deliver savings almost immediately. That early cash flow covers the debt service while the slower, more expensive building and fleet work matures. The fast wins carry the slow ones.

That's the whole trick. It isn't financial engineering. It's portfolio construction.

Bundle scopes with different payback curves into one contract, and the blended return funds work that could never have funded itself.

Four phases wasn't ambition.

It was the design.

Everyone tells you to start small.

Pilot it. Prove it. Do one building, see how it goes, expand later if the council's comfortable.

I understand the instinct. I think it's backwards.

Start small and you get small savings. Small savings can't cover the cost of the work. So the pilot needs its own funding, which means its own budget fight, which means it competes with the police contract and the road resurfacing — and it loses. Or it wins, barely, and you've spent your political capital proving a thing you already knew.

Two years later you're back at zero with a nicer boiler.

Hammond went the other direction. Four scopes at once, city-wide. And because the scope was big enough, the savings were big enough to pay for all of it.

The pilot is the risky version.

It's just risk you can't see, because it's spread across a decade instead of concentrated in a single vote.

Small isn't safe.

Small is slow, and slow is what deferred maintenance eats.

Week 5 — The Buildings / 23-Year Math
REEL THIS WEEK: R5 · Rob + Jeff — 23 yrs vs 3–5 · LI-CO
LI-CO Wed Leopardo Efficiency — Company Page
184 WORDS

Hammond replaced 4,800 streetlights.

The old ones were being replaced every 3 to 5 years. The new ones will be up there for 23.

Most people hear that and think: nice, better bulbs.

Look at what it actually does to a budget.

At a four-year cycle, 4,800 lights means roughly 1,200 replacements a year, forever. That's a crew, a bucket truck, a work order system, traffic control, and a parts inventory — permanently staffed, permanently funded, permanently on the list.

At 23 years, that line item doesn't shrink.

It disappears.

For two decades, nobody in Hammond writes a streetlight replacement work order. The bucket truck goes somewhere else. The crew does something that isn't undoing last year's work.

This is the part of lifecycle cost that never makes it into a council presentation, because the savings don't show up in the energy line. They show up in labor, in fleet hours, in inventory, and in five capital cycles that simply never happen.

The energy savings are real. They're also the smaller half.

The bigger half is all the work that no longer needs doing.

Week 6 — The Fleet / 119 Vehicles
REEL THIS WEEK: R6 · 119 vehicles, propane · IG
LI-CO Wed Leopardo Efficiency — Company Page
204 WORDS

"They can still run off of unleaded fuel — but now you have the option to run off of propane."

That's Jeff Sauline describing what happened to 119 of Hammond's highest-mileage vehicles. And that one word — option — is why the project works.

The objection we hear first, every time, is the same: what happens when the propane runs out? What if a cruiser is across town, mid-shift, and there's nowhere to fill up?

Nothing happens. It runs on unleaded.

These aren't propane vehicles. They're bi-fuel. Both systems are live. The vehicle carries a propane tank and its original gasoline tank, and it runs on either.

So the fleet never loses capability. A patrol car doesn't get less useful. A plow doesn't get stranded. Worst case, it runs exactly like it ran last year.

Best case — which is most of the time — it runs on the cheaper fuel.

That's what made this financeable. We didn't ask Hammond to bet on propane. We asked them to add propane as an option and take the spread whenever it's favorable.

You can't lose a bet you never had to make.

And 119 vehicles taking that spread, every day, for twenty years, is real money.

182 WORDS

Fleet is the line item every city underfunds.

Not out of negligence. Out of a very human logic: a truck that still starts isn't a problem yet.

So you run it another year. And another. The mileage climbs, the fuel economy drops, the repairs stack up — and none of it trips an alarm, because the truck still starts. Until the February morning it doesn't, and now you're buying a vehicle at whatever price the market feels like that week, out of a budget that didn't plan for it.

Hammond had 119 vehicles in that condition. High mileage, still running, quietly expensive.

They didn't replace them. They converted them.

Alternative fuel systems on the existing fleet — same trucks, cheaper to run — and 76 new vehicles added on top.

Here's what I want city leaders to take from that: your worst fleet assets aren't a cost problem waiting to happen.

They're the biggest savings opportunity you own.

The truck burning the most fuel is the one with the most to recover. The condition you've been avoiding is the condition that pays.

"Guaranteed" is the most abused word in our industry.

So let's be specific about what it means in Hammond's contract.

Heather Garay, Hammond's City Controller: "The equipment is guaranteed for 20 years for the city to use as they see fit. And I know the team, they try to be conservative with that number — and they expect those savings to be even greater."

Two things are happening in that sentence.

First: 20 years. Not a warranty period on a component. A performance guarantee on the savings, running two decades out.

Second — and this is the part that matters — she notes that we're conservative with the number.

That's deliberate. We are not incentivized to project the best case. We're incentivized to project a number we will not miss, because if we miss it, we pay the difference. Optimism costs us money. So we don't sell it.

The record: 59 projects. Zero missed guarantees.

But the record isn't the argument. Any firm can have a clean record right up until the year it doesn't.

The argument is what's written down for the year we don't.

We write the check. Not the city.

That's what the word is supposed to mean.

Eight weeks on Hammond. Here's the question it's been building toward.

Does your city qualify?

You're a strong candidate if you have:

Buildings you own. Aging HVAC, original lighting, no building automation. The more deferred, the better the return — the waste is the opportunity.

A fleet with mileage on it. High-mileage vehicles you keep running because replacing them isn't in the budget. Those are the best conversion candidates, not the worst.

Streetlights you own — not lease from the utility. Ownership is what makes the LED conversion yours to capture.

Scale. Not size — scale. Multiple scopes, and enough total waste that the savings can carry the debt service.

You're probably not a fit if your buildings were recently modernized, your fleet is new, your streetlights belong to the utility, or the total scope is small enough that the savings can't cover the financing. We'll tell you that in the audit. We'd rather tell you early than build you something that doesn't work.

Notice what isn't on the list.

Capital. A bond rating. Room in the general fund.

None of that determines whether you qualify.

The only thing that determines it is how much you're currently wasting.
